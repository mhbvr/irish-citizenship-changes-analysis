A closer look at the proposed citizenship changes.
Version 0.12 · 29 September 2026, 17:59 IST

Five years or eight?

The Scheme raises the residence needed for ordinary naturalisation from five years to eight, and the last two of those years must be continuous. For most future applicants it is the change they will feel first. [1]

Five years is common in the EU

The EU has no common rule. Seven member states let people apply after five years, counting any time needed first to obtain permanent residence: Belgium, France, Germany, Ireland (under the current rule), Luxembourg, Malta and the Netherlands. [2] No member state asks for less. These are general minimums, and each country counts residence differently. How each country was checked.

The Government’s announcement pointed to countries that require seven to ten years, among them Austria, Italy and Spain. It did not mention the seven member states that require five. [3]

Why eight?

The Scheme gives one reason. The longer period is meant to “establish a proportionate timeline that encourages steady economic and social integration before naturalisation”. [1]

It does not say why eight years is proportionate and five is not, which Irish problem the extra three years would solve, or how the number reflects Ireland’s own circumstances: a labour market that recruits abroad, a health service that depends on overseas-trained staff, and a national strategy to attract researchers. The people who naturalise here are, as the previous chapter shows, mostly already working and settled.

Uncertainty for people already here

The change falls on people who came under the current rules and planned their lives around them. Many are nurses, carers, doctors and IT specialists, recruited through a permit system that is still running: the State issued 31,044 employment permits in 2025, including renewals. [4] Ireland keeps inviting people to come and settle while telling them that the route to citizenship may become three years longer.

The Scheme has no transition rule for the residence period, and the start date will be set by ministerial order. Someone four and a half years into a five-year wait could find they need another three and a half, without knowing when the change takes effect. [1] The Government’s own statements add to the doubt. In a parliamentary answer the Minister of State said the new residence periods are “intended to apply from enactment of the new Bill”, and that people can apply under the current rules “only … until the new Bill is enacted”, while the Scheme ties the start to a later commencement order. Applicants cannot tell which date will count. [10] For many families, citizenship is the point at which they stop renewing immigration permissions and can plan for the long term. Moving that point, with no date and no protection for people part-way through, adds real stress to people who are working in Ireland and paying tax here.

Students and researchers would wait twelve years

Time on a student permission (Stamp 2) does not count as reckonable residence now, and would not count under the Scheme. [5] [6] A PhD student who spends four years on Stamp 2 and then takes a job in Ireland still has to build up the full residence period afterwards: nine years in total under the current rule, twelve under the proposal.

Until then, non-EU researchers travel on their original passports. Ireland is outside the Schengen area, and an Irish residence permission does not open it, so many need a separate Schengen visa for each conference, research visit or internship. [7] The European Commission itself acknowledges “long waiting times for Schengen visa appointments” in some places. [8] When an appointment cannot be found in time, the researcher misses the event, and the Irish university loses the chance to present its work.

Ireland’s own talent strategy, Global Citizens 2030, sets out to attract international researchers and PhD students. [9] Three more years before they can apply for citizenship works against that aim.
