A closer look at the proposed citizenship changes.
Version 0.11 · 29 September 2026, 13:06 IST

Who becomes an Irish citizen?

The Scheme is often discussed as if it were part of migration policy. As a migration tool it is indirect and hard to aim. Ireland already decides, directly, who can come here to work and who can join them. Citizenship comes years after that decision, so changing it mostly affects people who are already living here. To judge what the change would do, the first question is who actually becomes an Irish citizen.

The State already manages migration directly

For workers from outside the EEA, the State chooses which occupations can be filled through an employment permit, sets the minimum salary for each type of permit and can cap numbers in a given job. Family members need their own permission, and the rules set out who counts as family and how much the sponsor must earn. [1] [2] [3]

These rules are changed regularly. In May 2026 the Department of Enterprise revised its occupation lists and quotas to deal with shortages in housing, health and transport. [4] Asylum is a separate system again: whether someone qualifies for protection is decided under protection law, not through permits. [5]

Citizenship comes years later

Nobody can apply for citizenship on arrival. The ordinary route needs five years of reckonable residence, and in 2024 and 2025 a decision took a median of eight months. In practice, people become citizens close to six years after they arrive, by which time they have a job, a tax record and often children in Irish schools. [6] [7]

Raising the requirement to eight years would not change who arrives next year; the permit rules do that. It would change the prospects of people already here, and how Ireland compares with other countries for someone deciding where to build a career. The Scheme does not estimate either effect. [8] The rest of this chapter looks at the people it would reach.

Who became a citizen in 2025

The Department’s full-year figures for 2025 show who received a certificate of naturalisation. They count certificates issued, not new arrivals or applications received that year. [9]

Adults on the ordinary residence route received 64.6% of certificates. Children received 23.2%, and spouses and civil partners of Irish citizens (the Department’s “S15 residency” category) 9.8%. So one certificate in three went to a child or a partner. People with international protection, meaning refugees and people with subsidiary protection, received 2.4%. [10]

Certificates rose from 9,766 in 2021 to 29,919 in 2025, and India alone accounts for 27.5% of that increase. Most of the growth comes from the countries Ireland recruited from through the permit system in the late 2010s. Poland, by contrast, is close to where it was in 2016. [11]

Most came to work

The usual path for a worker from outside the EU is an employment permit first and citizenship at least five years later. The nationality figures follow that pattern. Eight of the ten non-EU nationalities with the most first-time permits in 2015–2020 are also among the ten with the most new citizens in 2021–2025. [12] [11]

India had 16,427 first-time permits in 2015–2020 and 14,669 certificates in 2021–2025. For Brazil, Pakistan, the Philippines and South Africa, certificates exceed the earlier permits, partly because partners and children naturalise as well. Nigeria and Syria look different: few of their citizens came on permits, and Syrian certificates are more likely to reflect international protection. The two datasets cannot be linked person by person, so the chart compares nationalities, not individuals. [12]

The permits themselves show what kind of work these are. Critical Skills permits are for highly skilled occupations in short supply; General permits cover other jobs the State has agreed can be filled from abroad. In 2024 the two together made up 36,646 of 39,398 permits (93.0%), counting renewals. [13] [2]

Health and social work is the largest sector by a wide margin and uses both routes. IT relies mostly on Critical Skills permits, while agriculture and hospitality rely mainly on General permits. Pharmaceutical, medical-device and electronics manufacturers recruit through the system too. [13]

The health service depends on them

5,031 Critical Skills permits in 2024 went to nurses, more than twice the 2,247 issued to programmers and software developers. [14] The professional registers show the same dependence.

On NMBI’s register at 1 June 2025, nurses and midwives educated outside the EU made up 48.8% of those in practice and 50.2% of those in patient-facing roles. The flow of new staff is even more lopsided: of the 7,120 people who registered for the first time between June 2023 and May 2024, 5,426 qualified outside the EU. [15] [16]

Doctors follow the same pattern. The Medical Council’s 2024 report found that 41.6% of clinically active doctors took their primary medical qualification outside Ireland: 13.8% in the EU or UK and 27.8% elsewhere, up from 23.3% two years earlier. These figures describe where people trained, not their nationality. [17]

What they contribute

Between the second quarters of 2021 and 2026, employment in Ireland grew by 457,100. Non-Irish citizens filled 215,900 of those jobs, or 47%, and their share of all employment rose from 16.6% to 21.5%. People who have already naturalised count as Irish in these figures, so they understate the contribution of migrants. [18]

Employment rates, ages 20–64, 2024
Area Non-EU citizens National citizens Gap
Ireland 77.4% 79.5% 2.1 points
EU 64.3% 76.7% 12.4 points

Among people aged 20–64, the employment rate of non-EU citizens in Ireland is 2.1 points below that of Irish citizens. Across the EU the gap is 12.4 points, and Ireland’s non-EU employment rate is 13.1 points above the EU average. [19] They are also well qualified: in Q2 2024, 67% of non-Irish residents aged 25–64 had a third-level qualification, compared with 53% of Irish residents. [20]

The latest review of the fiscal evidence for Ireland, published by the ESRI in June 2026, sums it up in one sentence: “immigrants in Ireland make a positive fiscal contribution, fully financing their share of public services.” [21]

Net contribution to the public finances in Ireland, OECD estimate, average of 2006–2018
Measure Born abroad Born in Ireland
Taxes paid, less benefits and services received +1.57% +2.87%
… and a share of police, transport, water and similar services +0.62% −2%
… and a share of defence, debt interest and all other public spending −0.21% −6.12%
Paid in for every €1 received (individual items) €1.52 €1.14

These OECD figures average 2006–2018. On the individual measure, immigrants paid in €1.52 for every euro they received in benefits and services, compared with €1.14 for people born in Ireland. They stayed in credit once a share of police, transport and other shared services was charged to them. People born in Ireland did not, and Ireland was the only country in the study where that happened. Only when defence, debt interest and all other public spending is divided per head does everyone come out behind, and the Irish-born more so. The ESRI attributes these negative figures to the deficits that followed 2008; the period does not include the budget surpluses of recent years. [19]

A separate EU estimate for 2015–2019 found a positive net contribution for every group, and a larger one for non-EU migrants than for people born in Ireland. International studies reviewed in the same report find that people who come through work routes, and higher-skilled migrants in particular, have the most positive fiscal impact. [19]

Use of unemployment payments points the same way. Between 2014 and 2024, 12% of people born in Asia received one, compared with 16% of people born in Ireland; the rate was higher, at 21%, for people born in the eastern EU and in Africa. [22] Nor is citizenship handed out quickly. The ESRI estimates that about 30% of non-EEA migrants who arrived since 2005 had become Irish citizens, with an annual acquisition rate of around 4%. [23]

What the evidence shows

Most people who become Irish citizens came here to work. Many arrived on employment permits as nurses, care workers, doctors and IT specialists, in jobs the State itself chose to recruit for abroad. Others came from the EU or the UK and needed no permit. They lived and worked here for five years, applied, and waited most of another year for a decision. Their partners and children applied with them: one certificate in three in 2025 went to a spouse, civil partner or child.

They are, in large part, the skilled professionals Ireland competes with other countries to attract. They work at close to the same rate as Irish citizens, they are more likely to hold a third-level qualification, and on the best available estimates they pay more into the public finances than they take out.

These are the people the Scheme would affect. It does not touch the permit and visa rules that decide who comes to Ireland. It lengthens and complicates the route for people who are already working and settling here, and it changes the offer Ireland makes to the people it will need to recruit in future, many of them for the health service. The Government has not estimated what that would cost in recruitment or retention. [8]
