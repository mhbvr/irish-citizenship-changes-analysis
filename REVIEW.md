# Critical review of “Analysis of the proposed amendment to the citizenship law”

Reviewed 29 September 2026, after the rewrite that removed recommendations, added a
conclusion and expanded the overview. Written from the point of view of a sceptical
reader — a senator’s adviser, a Department official or a journalist checking the claims.
This file is not part of the website.

## Overall verdict

The site is now tighter and reads as an argument rather than a policy memo. Its
strongest material is concrete and hard to dispute: the undecided regulations, the
missing transition rule, the Schedule 1 paperwork offences, and the nationality data.
Its weakest points are where a correlation is presented as a finding, where evidence
about “migrants” in general is used to describe the narrower group the Scheme affects,
and where awkward facts are acknowledged on one page but not carried through to the
overview and conclusion. A hostile reader would target those first.

## Major issues, in order of risk

### 1. “Most new citizens came to work” is an inference, not a measured fact

The claim appears in chapter 1, the overview and the conclusion. The evidence is
nationality-level: first-time permits in 2015–2020 line up with certificates in
2021–2025 for eight of the ten largest non-EU nationalities. Chapter 1 states the
limit (“the chart compares nationalities, not individuals”), but the overview and
conclusion state the conclusion without it. The Department does not publish
recipients’ previous permissions, so nobody can prove the share. A critic can point
out that Brazil’s certificates exceed its permits, that students and spouses are in
the same numbers, and that 64.6% on the ordinary route includes people who came on
many kinds of permission.

**Risk:** the site’s central premise can be called unproven.

### 2. The “S15 residency = spouses” reading is not sourced to the Department

The site now states that “S15 residency” is the route for spouses and civil partners
of Irish citizens. That comes from the project owner’s knowledge, not from the
Department’s table, which does not define the label. It is plausible (the ordinary
route is already listed separately as “Standard Adult”), but section 15A, not section
15, is the spouse route. If an official says otherwise, the “one certificate in three
goes to a child or partner” statistic loses a third of its weight.

**Risk:** a factual challenge on a headline number. A parliamentary question or the
Department’s own definition would settle it.

### 3. Evidence about all migrants is used for the people the Scheme affects

Several headline figures are broader than the affected group:

- **43%–65% of the net rise in employment since 2021** (47% on a Q2 2021 base) was among
  non-Irish citizens. That includes EU and UK
  citizens, who need no permit and are less affected by most of the Scheme.
- **43.4% of doctors qualified outside Ireland** includes 13.9% who qualified in the
  EU or UK.
- **Nurses and midwives** are counted by where they trained, not by nationality; some
  are Irish citizens who trained abroad, and some are already naturalised.
- **The fiscal figures** describe all immigrants (OECD, 2006–2018; JRC, 2015–2019),
  not non-EU workers approaching citizenship. The EU estimate is the one that
  separates non-EU migrants, and it is six years old.

None of these are wrong as stated, but the overview uses them to make a point about
the people who naturalise. A careful reader will notice the gap.

### 4. The EU comparison now partly works against the argument

After counting time to permanent residence, the EU average is about eight years —
roughly what Ireland proposes — and the two most recent changes (Sweden, Portugal)
went up, not down. The residence chapter reports this honestly, but the overview and
conclusion still lean on “Germany, France and the Netherlands ask for five years”.
That is true, but selective; a reader who has seen the table will ask why the
comparison stops there. Eleven of the 27 country figures are also not confirmed on
an official page, and Romania may be understated (possibly about 13 years).

**Risk:** the Government can answer “we are moving to the European average”. The
stronger point, already on the residence page, is that the Scheme gives no Irish
reason for eight years and no evidence about its effect.

### 5. The biggest group affected by one change is almost absent

Head 9 would stop time on temporary protection counting as residence. That affects
tens of thousands of Ukrainian residents, many in work, and it is one of the
clearest cases of people losing years already lived here. The site mentions it only
in a list on the overview and in one sentence of the conclusion. The research folder
has the CSO data on Ukrainian employment (UA29, UA30) that would support a short
section.

### 6. The PhD example needs its caveat back

“A PhD graduate … would wait twelve years” assumes four years entirely on a student
permission followed by a job in Ireland. Researchers employed under a hosting
agreement or another work permission can accrue reckonable residence during their
research. The previous conclusion said so (“Employed researchers with other
permissions have different histories”); the rewrite dropped it, and the residence
page never had it. Without the caveat, the example can be called exaggerated.

### 7. The Schengen visa point is tangential

The visa difficulty is real and now sourced, but it is not caused by the Scheme: it
exists under the current five-year rule too. The Scheme only lengthens the period
before a researcher can escape it. The page implies more than that. One sentence
making the link explicit (“three more years of this”) would make it defensible.

## Chapter notes

- **Overview.** Clear and much better focused on uncertainty. The section on what the
  changes mean for Ireland is now about twice its former length and argues from Irish interests. It repeats three
  statistics that appear again in chapter 1 and the conclusion; by the conclusion the
  reader has seen “nearly half of nurses” and “47% of jobs” three times.
- **1. Who becomes a citizen?** About 2,500 words against roughly 300–550 for every
  other chapter. It carries the whole evidence case, but its length makes the site
  feel lopsided, and the contribution section (jobs, fiscal, welfare) reads like a
  separate chapter. The OECD table is fair and well explained.
- **2. Five years or eight?** Strong and well sourced. The “Why eight?” section is the
  best single argument on the site. The press-release quotation (“7 to 10 years”)
  could not be re-checked on gov.ie, which blocked automated access.
- **3. Language and civics.** Tight. The capacity model is transparent but rests on
  chosen assumptions (two tests each, 20% retakes, 200 days, one room); a critic can
  dismiss it as invented. The research folder shows 87.3% of residents who speak
  another language at home speak English well or very well (95% of Indian citizens);
  that would show the test solves little and is not used.
- **4. Income and support.** The mean-versus-median comparison argues against a
  threshold nobody has proposed. It illustrates why the choice matters, but it can be
  read as a straw man. The strongest point is the last paragraph (no protection for
  maternity, illness or caring; the lookback reaching back before the rules existed).
- **5. Offences.** The Schedule 1 examples are specific and effective. The theft
  comparison is legally subtle and may confuse lay readers. “Committed”, not
  “convicted”, is the sharper point and is made on the overview but not here.
- **6. Revocation.** Now brief and factual. “No case has been published” is an argument
  from absence; the Department can reply that security cases are not published by
  nature.
- **7. Conclusion.** Reads as a real conclusion now. The last paragraph carries the
  argument well. It introduces the Head 4 children point in more detail than any
  chapter does; that material has no chapter of its own.

## Style

- The recommendation language has gone; the only “should” left is the author’s
  view stated on the overview, which is appropriate.
- Machine-writing tics are mostly gone. Remaining patterns: some paragraphs end on a
  neat summarising sentence (“That is the main cost of the proposal as published…”);
  frequent “It does not say…” constructions in a row on the residence and overview
  pages; and very even paragraph lengths across chapters.
- The tone is measured. Since version 0.10 the site presents itself as an individual view
  rather than “independent analysis”, which is more credible for what is plainly an
  advocacy piece.

## Facts and housekeeping to check

- The sidebar and footer say “Checked 26 September 2026”, but sources were rechecked
  on 28–29 September (Sweden, Portugal, the EU table, Schengen, the visa strategy).
- Eleven EU country figures rest on secondary sources (see the sources page).
- Romania: 8 years of “domicile” may mean 13 in practice; unresolved.
- Poland: the Office for Foreigners page could not be read automatically; the
  five-year permanent-residence rule comes from search results quoting it.
- The press-release quotation comes from earlier research notes, not a live check.
- “S15 residency” meaning (see issue 2).
- Median processing time of eight months is for 2024 and 2025, and the site says so;
  applications in 2026 may take longer.

## What I would fix first

1. Put the caveat from chapter 1 (“nationalities, not individuals”) into the overview
   and conclusion wherever “most came to work” appears.
2. Confirm the meaning of “S15 residency” from an official source.
3. Add a short section on temporary protection (Ukraine) with the CSO employment data.
4. Restore the PhD caveat and tie the Schengen point to the extra three years.
5. Narrow the headline statistics to non-EU residents where the data allow it, or say
   when a figure covers all migrants.
6. Update the “checked” dates.
