A closer look at the proposed citizenship changes.
Version 0.15 · 26 October 2026, 19:10 GMT

A permanent bar for a paperwork offence?

The proposed Schedule is not confined to identity fraud or illegal entry. It also reaches administrative duties. Under Heads 5 and 6, committing a listed offence would create a separate citizenship bar with no time limit in the wording. [1]

Three concrete examples

Existing duties included through Schedule 1
Duty Legal provision
Notify a new home address within 48 hours if it was not notified in advance. Immigration Act 2004, sections 9(2)(c) and 9(8). [2]
Notify the current address when absent from the registered residence for more than one month. Sections 9(2)(d) and 9(8). [2]
Produce a passport or equivalent document and, where registered, a registration certificate on a lawful demand. Section 12. A reasonable-cause defence applies. [3]

These examples concern people to whom the duties apply. They are not a claim that every late update or missing document automatically proves an offence. They show the range of conduct covered by the proposed bar.

Good character does not demand perfection

In Hussain v Minister for Justice [2011] IEHC 171, paragraph 15, Hogan J. said the standard was “reasonable standards of civic responsibility as gauged by reference to contemporary values”. Applicants could not be held to behaviour unrealistically higher than that expected of Irish people. [4]

The judgment also permits consideration of serious wrongdoing without a conviction, provided fair procedures are followed. The issue is proper evidence and an individual assessment, not an entitlement to ignore misconduct. [4]

No discretion on good character, even for children

Head 8 would add a new subsection (1B) to section 16: the good-character condition in sections 15(1)(b), 15A(1)(b) and 15B(1)(b) “cannot be waived in any circumstances”. That covers the ordinary route, spouses and civil partners of Irish citizens, and minors born in the State. [1]

Under section 15B, the condition does not apply to a child under 14 unless the child is charged with or convicted of murder, manslaughter, rape or aggravated sexual assault. From 14 it does apply, but section 15B(4) lets the Minister waive it where the particular circumstances of the minor make that appropriate. Head 8 would take that power away. Section 16 also lets the Minister dispense with section 15 conditions for some applicants: people of Irish descent or associations, the minor children of naturalised citizens, refugees and stateless people. As drafted, the new subsection would also stop good character being dispensed with on those routes. The Scheme does not say how this applies to younger children on them. [5] [1]

The explanatory note gives one reason: “to ensure consistency and to reflect the policy position that all applicants must be of good character”. It refers only to section 15(1)(b). It does not mention children, the existing waiver for those aged 14 to 17, or any case in which that waiver was misused. [1]

The Irish Human Rights and Equality Commission, in its October 2026 submission on the Scheme, says it should be considered whether this is compliant with the rights of the child. The Commission has previously connected naturalisation to the State’s duties under Article 7 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, which includes the right to acquire a nationality. [6]

The change does not raise the standard expected of a teenager; it removes the room to apply it with judgment. Where a 15- or 16-year-old falls short, the Minister could no longer weigh the child’s age, maturity, family situation or the time since an incident, which is what section 15B(4) allows now. A refusal could leave a child with a different status from parents and siblings who become citizens. Minors received 6,949 certificates in 2025, but the published figures do not show how many were 14 or older, or how many needed a waiver. [5] [7]

The bar is not ordered by seriousness

A scheduled registration offence receives an express bar with no expiry, while a non-scheduled offence such as ordinary theft remains subject to the good-character assessment. That does not guarantee citizenship after theft; it shows that the Schedule’s treatment is not simply ordered by seriousness. [1] [5]

The Scheme says nothing about how an offence would be proved, how far back it can reach, or how an old breach could be reviewed. As written, an address change notified late and later corrected would carry the same permanent consequence as deliberate fraud. [1]
